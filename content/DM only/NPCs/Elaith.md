**Goal:** 

She was a young priestess of the Lady, twenty-six, chosen by her order for what they told her was a sacred honor — a ritual of communion, a chance to touch the goddess directly. She was devout enough to not ask too many questions. The order needed a living anchor inside the Belly to stabilize a ritual being performed from outside. She was the anchor. The ritual failed, or succeeded at something she wasn't told about, or was never really meant to bring her back. She doesn't know which. She has had thirty years to think about it and she still doesn't know, and that uncertainty is its own wound.

She figured out what she was for gradually, over the first few years, piecing it together from what she knew about the ritual's mechanics and what she experienced from inside. By the time she understood it fully she was already too changed by the Belly to feel the rage cleanly. It came out sideways instead — in the meticulous, almost punishing thoroughness of her documentation, in the controlled way she speaks, in the very particular silence she produces when someone mentions faith or divine purpose.

She is not bitter in any legible way. She is something quieter and harder than bitter. She has had thirty years to alchemize it into something she can live with, and she has, and what she lives with is not comfortable to be around.

_What she knows:_ She arrived with significant theological knowledge of the Lady and has spent thirty years observing the Belly directly. She knows more about how the entity's cancer behaves, spreads, and can be temporarily avoided than anyone the party will meet. She also knows — because she worked it out — roughly what kind of ritual the party performed to get here, and therefore roughly what they were told, and therefore roughly what they weren't told.

_What she does with that knowledge:_ She doesn't volunteer it immediately. She helps the party practically, gives them what they need, feeds them if they need feeding. She watches them. At some point — when she's decided they deserve to know, or when she's decided it's cruel not to tell them — she asks them a very specific question about the ritual they performed. The question will tell them, if they're paying attention, that she already knows the answer.

_The scene:_ She doesn't expose whoever sent them dramatically. She just says, quietly, that she was also told her ritual was an honor. And then she says nothing else and lets them sit with it. She's not doing it to hurt them. She's doing it because she spent thirty years wishing someone had said that to her before she went in.

_What she wants:_ She has stopped wanting things in the large sense. In the small sense she wants the other two people in her camp to be alright. She also wants — and this is something she would not say out loud — to know that her being here did something. That the anchor held. That whatever she was used for mattered. If the party's ritual works she will never know, because she'll still be here. But she might be able to feel it.

_The hope in her:_ She kept the other two alive. She has maintained her documentation for thirty years with no expectation anyone would ever read it. She has kept faith with something — not the order that sent her, not the Lady as she was told to understand her, but something. Call it rigor. Call it the refusal to stop paying attention. It is the most durable form of hope and the least comfortable to witness.

_What makes her unsettling:_ She is completely calm. Not the calm of someone who has healed — the calm of someone who has finished grieving and found that on the other side of grief there is just clarity. She sees the party very accurately. She makes no assumptions about whether they will succeed. And when she looks at them there is a moment — brief, not unkind — where they might feel like she is already remembering them in the past tense.
