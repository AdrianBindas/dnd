
### **The God-Machine's Angels — Chronicles of Darkness**

Chronicles of Darkness

Angels aren't just powerful NPCs; they often operate according to **Infrastructure**—occult conspiratorial systems of locations, people, objects, and events.

The interesting part is that fighting the angel may be the _least effective solution_. Players can instead:

- identify what occult machine the angel is maintaining,
- sabotage a seemingly unrelated component,
- exploit the angel's specific purpose,
- or make its mission logically impossible.

**Monster as a systems puzzle** is something I wish more games stole.


### **The Unchained's Demons — Demon: The Descent**

The player characters are demons, but the antagonistic angels and Infrastructure can have powers that feel almost like **administrative privileges over reality**.

An enemy might not "cast fireball." Instead, it can effectively make:

> _This door has always been locked._

Or manipulate systems of identity, surveillance, bureaucracy, and causality.

The horror comes from realizing you're fighting something that controls the **metadata of the world**, not merely its physical objects.


### **The Tyrant Wyrm and other colossal threats — 13th Age**

I know you said no D&D, and **13th Age is adjacent enough that I'll keep this brief**, but its monster design is worth mentioning because of **escalation mechanics and triggered powers**.

Many monsters have attacks that trigger based on:

- odd/even die results,
- natural attack rolls,
- whether the battle is escalating,
- specific conditions in the fiction.

This makes enemies feel less like static stat blocks and more like **combat engines with states**.

The useful design lesson is: **the monster's behavior can emerge from the dice rather than GM decision-making.**


### **Ultra and Elite enemies — Lancer**

Lancer

_Lancer_ has some excellent boss design.

Important enemy templates include things like:

### **Ultra**

Boss units can have:

- multiple turns or activations,
- extra structure,
- powerful reactions,
- special phase-like mechanics.

This solves the classic RPG problem where five PCs simply delete the boss before it meaningfully acts.

### **Elite**

Effectively gives an enemy more action economy and durability without merely multiplying its HP.

The key lesson is:

> **Bosses need more decisions, not just more hit points.**


### **Corruption monsters — Symbaroum**

Symbaroum

One of the coolest ideas in _Symbaroum_ is that the supernatural is tied to **Corruption**.

Using magic and interacting with dangerous forces can accumulate corruption, and enough of it can transform a character.

This creates monsters with a particularly nasty dynamic:

> **The thing you're fighting might be what you become.**

That's excellent theming because the monster mechanic applies equally to PCs.

The threat isn't external. It's a possible **future state of the character sheet**.


### **Archetypal and obsession-driven entities — Unknown Armies**

Unknown Armies

_Unknown Armies_ is full of entities and phenomena where **symbolic logic matters more than biological logic**.

A supernatural threat may be vulnerable because of:

- its archetype,
- its personal obsession,
- ritual symbolism,
- collective belief,
- the narrative role it occupies.

The fun part is that players can sometimes beat the supernatural by understanding its **metaphysics and psychology** rather than bringing bigger guns.
